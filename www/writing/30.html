30

Somewhere between the things I thought life would be, and the things it has become.

There's a note in the cloud, written by me in 2018 (22). It's titled Reflections, and the contents of the page are:

If there's anything I've learned from my brief stint on this earth, it's that I'm bad at it.

In 2023 (27), while going through a breakup, I also wrote:

I don’t know what life's going to end up looking like, but I think I should do everything I can to make sure I want to continue it.
You're never going to get on 100% all of the time. You will create scars and some of them will not be fully resolved. But you can do your best to keep going and be the supportive force in each other's lives. That is the backbone of love.

Looking back through these notes is a trip down memory lane. It wells emotion, but there's enough distance now for it to feel like a past life, with lessons learned.

Some days I resonate with these sentiments. Some days I forget them.

They're not issues I thought I would still be wrestling with as I enter this decade of my life. But truth be told, I didn't imagine I'd be alive to finish my degree.

That's right. I completed tertiary education. It's easy to skip over remembering this achievement after a decade.

It's also easy to forget that I have been writing as a form of catharsis and elation for over 15 years. There's a collection of Moleskines that will never be read, or discarded, somewhere in the garage.

I've spoken with a few about how unprepared one can feel after 25. When you've ticked a few boxes in the checklist of the American dream, life becomes what you make of it.

And with the unpreparedness that accompanies the short-term viewpoint for life in one's youth, time is daunting.

Growing up with pseudo-millennial optimism and a Christian worldview really tricked me into thinking that life would yield a degree, job, house, wife, children, and dog at the ripe old age of roughly 23.

There's a beauty in the naivety of that hope, and in where one finds themselves after shattering through many of those elements.

Years on from writing those quotes, life is very quiet in the evenings. Peaceful at many a time, but also surfaces an internal weight on the organs like nothing else.

This year I was lucky enough to have rekindled a dormant part of me. It took a leap of faith to let someone into my life again, and proved how wonderful it can be to have an outlet for the hopelessly romantic part of my soul to breathe again.

As it happens, though a strong connection, life can throw the right people at you at the wrong time.

Life also thinks it's funny to have two women decide you're wonderful, but they need space for themselves, very shortly after returning from Italy.

Must be something in the wine.

I could tell you that I have no regrets, and my mind is filled with lackadaisical whimsy 24/7. It's not. But I don't have regrets.

It takes a while to build up the character to realise that to regret is to mourn a path not travelled, and there's no saying how rewarding that path may have been.

To hold onto jaded spite and woe serves nothing but to hurt oneself, even though the misery can feel like an old friend at times.

"I see it all perfectly; there are two possible situations - one can either do this or that. My honest opinion and my friendly advice is this: do it or do not do it - you will regret both." — Søren Kierkegaard

When faced with a fork in the road, it's easy to romanticise the destination each end may yield.

It's much harder to romanticise the journey after the cold of the rain has seeped into your bones en route.

But I'm practising.

Sitting with all that my heart presents to my mind, reasoning, being thankful for what has occurred, and daring myself to be optimistic for what will come.

Just this evening, as I write, I deboned a chicken in the kitchen, of the house I am lucky to own and call a home, with my favourite songs humming in the background, my dog — who loves me far more than I will ever deserve — at my feet, looking into the garden as birds chirp in the pittosporum and the spring sunset glimmers through the leaves of the hedge.

Life is good if you prioritise looking for what you appreciate about it.

Just this year I remembered that it is rewarding to show and be shown love, to share your life with another.

Adventures around the island have been had in an abundance few can afford, and new friends with welcome and open arms have reminded me that people are good and worth your time.

Embracing nostalgia, making things with your hands, writing, a glass of wine, burning a new recipe on the stove, sitting on the hills taking photos, revelling in music released from bands you've been following (and lucky enough to see) for the last decade, ice cream, laughter, raucous 270-degree crankshaft exhaust notes, speeding tickets, cold nights riding motorbikes in fishnets, the smell of freshly ground coffee permeating the house, vices that spin your head, dates, sex, warm socks, old books, markets, swimming, vinyls scratching as they come to an end, the smell of brake cleaner, bagels, cheese, keeping something other than yourself alive, stories.

How lucky am I to make so many fuckups and still be here to experience it all.

Negatively comparing oneself to others — fostering regret over not having the same experiences — is such a disingenuous way to negate all accomplishments of a life thus far.

Everyone has a story. Everyone has priorities.

The priorities we each hold dictate the stories we create and the achievements they yield.

I'm still remembering to appreciate that though not all things, life has gone fairly according to plan.

I think I know what I wish to prioritise going into the next decade.

It'll be quieter and more affordable than starting a family, and even for that small fact I am grateful.

If you read this, thank you.
